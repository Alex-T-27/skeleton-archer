▚ Summer 2026 · Robotics Build

SKELETON ARCHER

Two robot arms, Minecraft-skeleton style, that find a target anywhere across a 180° arc and loose a single toy arrow at it — on your command.

Scope locked: ONE shot · looks-first (it must read as a skeleton archer) · close range · toy arrow · printed bullseye on cardboard.

01The tricks that make it doable

The naive version eats a whole summer. These four decisions cut out the brutal parts while keeping the look.

▲ Keep the elastic LIGHT

The draw arm pulls the string to tension itself — but hobby servos are weak and overheat holding a static load. Close range + a toy arrow needs barely any force, so a light rubber band is enough. The moment you want a "real" snappy bow, the servo can't do it. Stay light.

▲ One arm holds, one arm draws

A real archer doesn't pull the string with both hands. Bow arm holds steady, draw arm pulls + releases. Only one release point — which kills the dual-arm sync nightmare (two grippers letting go at the exact same millisecond). Also looks more like an archer.

▲ A rotating base does the aiming

Instead of re-solving arm angles for every target position, put the whole archer on a turntable. The base swivels the body to face the target; the arms always perform the same fixed draw motion. Aiming and arm-coordination stay separate problems.

▲ Pretty target ≠ what the camera sees

Detecting a colored bullseye reliably is finicky (ovals at an angle, lighting, scale). So: print the nice bullseye for the look, and stick a small AprilTag on it — a chunky black-and-white square the camera locks onto instantly for rock-solid position + angle.

02The five parts

PART 01
Launcher
Light elastic "bow", a toy arrow, and a clean release.
PART 02
Two arms
Bow arm holds steady · draw arm pulls & releases.
PART 03
Aiming base
Turntable swivels the body to face the target.
PART 04
Vision brain
Webcam + AprilTag → the target's angle. Your wheelhouse.
PART 05
Sequencer
ROS 2 ties it: detect → aim → draw → hold → shoot → release.

03Build order

Do the cheap, arm-free stuff first so you're never blocked waiting on hardware.

P0

Prove the launch by hand NO ARM

Rubber band, a dowel, something arrow-shaped, your hand as the release. Does a light projectile fly roughly straight at a board? That's the whole physics risk in one cheap test. (On hold — no rubber band right now.)

P1

Vision → target angle NEXT

Webcam points at a printed AprilTag; a script detects it and outputs where it is (left/center/right, then an actual angle). Pure software, zero hardware. New skill to learn: AprilTag detection + turning a detection into an aim angle.

P2

Full sequence in simulation NO ARM

Model the archer in sim (Pokie / Isaac) and get the whole detect → aim → draw → release sequence working virtually before any real arm exists.

P3

Real arms (friend lends a hand)

Print + wire two arms (SO-ARM100 style: 3D-printed frame + servos). Then, in order: one arm draws & releases the real elastic → add the bow arm → add the rotating base.

P4

Integrate + dress it up

Wire the vision aim into the real base, then add the skeleton styling so it finally looks like a Minecraft archer. Tune until it lands ~8 of 10 practice tries.

04The one risk to manage

■ The arms are the long pole

If the whole project can't move until two physical arms exist and work — and your friend gets busy — your summer stalls. The fix is the build order above: the vision brain (P1) and the full sequence (P2) get built and tested in simulation while the arms are being made. You're never idle waiting on someone else.
